# Key question, key finding, take-home message

**Key question:** How has the choice between mechanical and biological valve prostheses evolved in the public health system of a middle-income country with a high burden of rheumatic disease?

**Key finding:** Over 26 years and 305 566 admissions, mechanical prostheses remained about one-third of implants, with substantial between-hospital variation (median odds ratio 3.15) and an increase in patients younger than 60 years after 2020.

**Take-home message:** Unlike high-income countries, Brazil's public system did not experience a marked decline in mechanical prostheses, and prosthesis choice varied widely between hospitals and regions.

# ABSTRACT

**OBJECTIVES:** Guidelines favour mechanical prostheses in younger patients, yet national data on prosthesis choice
in middle-income countries with a high burden of rheumatic disease are lacking. We describe 26 years of surgical
prosthesis selection in Brazil's public health system (SUS).

**METHODS:** Nationwide cohort of all SUS admissions billing a heart-valve prosthesis (2000–2025), identified from
hospital-billing microdata. Prosthesis type was taken from the billed device code. Trends, a multilevel model of
mechanical-prosthesis use in adults with aortic or mitral replacement (2008–2025) and an interrupted time-series
analysis (break at March 2020) were performed.

**RESULTS:** Of 305 566 admissions, the mechanical share remained 28–36% throughout. In 143 359 adults
(2008–2025), mechanical use was associated with younger age (40–49 vs 60–64 years: OR 3.39, 95% CrI 3.29–3.49),
coded rheumatic aetiology (OR 1.18) and region (South vs Southeast: OR 2.35; Northeast: OR 0.38). Between-hospital
variation was large (MOR 3.15; ICC 0.31); 98 of 171 hospitals lay outside 99.8% funnel limits. In patients aged
18–59 years, mechanical use rose after 2020 (aortic 45.3% to 52.1%; mitral 43.7% to 52.4%, 2018–2019 vs
2022–2025; level change OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.28–1.77), within hospitals (OR 1.64) and in all sensitivity analyses.

**CONCLUSIONS:** Unlike high-income countries, Brazil's public system did not experience a marked decline in
mechanical prostheses. Prosthesis choice varied widely between hospitals and regions, and mechanical use in patients
younger than 60 years increased after 2020, for reasons that these data cannot establish.

# INTRODUCTION

The choice between a mechanical and a biological prosthesis balances lifelong anticoagulation against structural
valve deterioration and reintervention [1,2]. European and American guidelines favour a mechanical prosthesis in
younger patients, with age thresholds of 60 (aortic) and 65 years (mitral) [1] or 50 (aortic) and 65 years
(mitral) [2], and favour a biological prosthesis when anticoagulation of adequate quality is unlikely and in women
contemplating pregnancy [1,2]. The Brazilian guideline does not define age thresholds [3]. Observational studies
suggest a survival advantage of mechanical prostheses in younger patients [4–8], and randomized trials from the
pre-transcatheter era showed less reoperation and more bleeding with mechanical valves [9,10].

In high-income countries, the use of mechanical prostheses has fallen steeply. In the United States, mechanical
prostheses in patients aged 50–70 years declined from 45% to 17% (aortic) and from 60% to 29% (mitral) between
2008 and 2017 [11], and also declined in women of reproductive age [12]. Similar trends were reported elsewhere
[13,14]. Whether this shift extends to middle-income countries, where rheumatic disease predominates and access to
anticoagulation monitoring is uneven [15], is unknown. The 2025 European guideline notes that the impact of the
aetiology of valve disease on prosthesis choice "remains unexplored" [1].

In Brazil, the public Unified Health System (SUS) funds most cardiac surgery. National studies have described
mortality after valve surgery [16,17], but none reported the prosthesis type, which the largest cohort listed as
unavailable [17]; single-centre series reported 70% biological prostheses [18,19]. We used the device billed in
each admission to describe prosthesis choice in the SUS over 26 years, its determinants, between-hospital
variation and a change after 2020. To our knowledge, this is the first national description of prosthesis type in a
middle-income country with a high burden of rheumatic disease.

# PATIENTS AND METHODS

## Data source and study design

This retrospective cohort used the national hospital-billing database of the SUS (Sistema de Informações
Hospitalares, SIH), which records every publicly funded admission. Two linked files were used: the reduced admission
file (RD; demographics, diagnoses, hospital, dates, in-hospital death) and the professional-services file (SP), which
lists every billed item, including orthoses, prostheses and special materials (OPM). Files were obtained from a
public mirror of DATASUS with checksum verification (healthbr-data, data.sidneybissoli.com) because the official FTP
server was not reachable; [details and file coverage, Supplementary Material]. The study followed the RECORD
statement [20].

## Study population and prosthesis type

We included all admissions from January 2000 to December 2025 in which at least one heart-valve prosthesis was
billed. Prosthesis type was defined by the billed OPM code: biological (with or without stent) or mechanical
(bileaflet or tilting disc). Since 2008 these are SIGTAP codes 0702040541, 0702040550, 0702040568 and 0702040576;
for 2000–2007, the equivalent codes of the former SIH procedure table (93323131, 93329156, 93323158, 93323166) were
identified in the official national equivalence table (TUNEP) and verified for continuity across the 2007–2008
table change. Multiple-procedure admissions were included. Admissions billing both types (n = 1021) were excluded
from type-specific analyses. Characteristics of included and excluded admissions are shown in Table S8.

## Valve position, aetiology and covariates

Valve position and aetiology were inferred from ICD-10 codes (aortic: I35, I06, Q23.0–Q23.1; mitral: I34, I05,
Q23.2–Q23.3; rheumatic: I05–I09). Q22.5 and I38 were ignored in adults because they were used as default codes in
some hospitals. Endocarditis was defined by I33 in any diagnostic field. Admissions with two or more prostheses,
tricuspid or pulmonary codes, or no valve code were excluded from position-specific analyses (Figure S1). Before
2008 only two diagnostic fields exist, so position-specific series start in 2002 (indeterminate position 42% in
2000–2001). Covariates were age, sex, admission type (elective or urgent), region, hospital annual volume and period.

## Validation

Prosthesis recording was compared with published single-centre series and with hospital-level completeness (OPM
billed in 93–98% of valve-replacement admissions). Position coding was audited by hospital-year; hospitals using a
single default code for ≥70% of admissions were identified (163–199 hospital-years, 5.1% of admissions, mostly
I05), as were hospitals with <80% OPM recording (Supplementary Material). Coded rheumatic aetiology was lower than
in a reference centre (32% vs 58.5%) and should be read as a minimum.

## Statistical analysis

Proportions of mechanical prostheses were described by year, age group, sex, position and region. Determinants of
mechanical use in adults (2008–2025) were estimated with a mixed-effects logistic model with a random intercept for
hospital (hospitals with ≥30 admissions), fitted by variational Bayes; results are ORs with 95% credible intervals.
Between-hospital variation was summarized by the MOR and ICC [21,22]. Case-mix-adjusted hospital proportions
(2020–2025, age 18–59 years) were displayed in a funnel plot with 95% and 99.8% limits [23].

The change after 2020 was assessed in patients aged 18–59 years by (i) a segmented binomial model of monthly
proportions with calendar-month terms and heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent standard errors
(12 lags), with the break at March 2020 (onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil); the break point that minimized
the deviance (May 2020) and placebo break points in 2014, 2016 and 2018 were examined as sensitivity analyses; (ii) logistic models comparing 2022–2025 with 2018–2019, adjusted for position, aetiology, sex,
age, admission type and region, with hospital fixed effects in hospitals active in both periods; and (iii) annual
counts of each prosthesis type. Sensitivity analyses excluded hospital-years with a default diagnostic code,
hospitals with <80% OPM recording, 2008–2009 and endocarditis, separately and combined. Analyses used Python 3.13
(pandas, statsmodels). Code is available in a public repository (see Data availability statement).

Long-term outcomes were not analysed because the public files lack a personal identifier; exploratory, SUS-captured
reintervention and complication data are shown in Table S7 only, with their limitations.

# RESULTS

## Cohort

Between 2000 and 2025, 305 566 admissions billed a heart-valve prosthesis (9351–15 779 per year; 80 245 in
2000–2007 and 225 321 in 2008–2025). The analysis cohort for 2008–2025 comprised 143 359 adults with aortic or
mitral replacement and a single prosthesis type (Figure 1; excluded admissions in Table S8): median age 58 years, 49.4% women, 53.8% mitral, 47.7%
with a coded rheumatic aetiology (Table 1). Patients receiving a mechanical prosthesis were younger (median 51 vs
62 years) and more often had mitral and rheumatic disease. In-hospital mortality was 8.6%.

## Trends, 2000–2025

The overall mechanical share was 33–35% in 2000–2010, fell to 28% in 2016–2017 and rose to 36% in 2022 (Table S2).
In patients aged 18–59 years, mechanical aortic prostheses accounted for 47.3% in 2002–2007, 44.3% in 2013–2017 and
52.1% in 2022–2025; mechanical mitral prostheses increased from 37.8% to 41.1% and 52.4%, the highest level in the
series (Figure 2). In patients aged ≥60 years, mechanical prostheses remained at 12–20%.

## Determinants and hospital variation

Age was the main determinant of mechanical use (Table 2): compared with 60–64 years, ORs were 2.92 (18–39 years),
3.39 (40–49) and 2.29 (50–59), and 0.09 at ≥70 years. Coded rheumatic aetiology (OR 1.18, 95% CrI 1.15–1.20) and
higher hospital volume (≥100/year: OR 1.18) were associated with mechanical use; endocarditis (OR 0.54) and urgent
admission (OR 0.90) with biological use. Regional differences were marked: South OR 2.35 and Centre-West 1.13,
Northeast 0.38 and North 0.35, compared with the Southeast. Hospital variation exceeded that of most patient
factors (MOR 3.15; ICC 0.31; 239 hospitals). Among 171 hospitals with ≥30 admissions of patients aged 18–59 years
in 2020–2025, the adjusted mechanical share ranged from 15% to 87% (10th–90th percentile), and 49 hospitals lay
above and 49 below the 99.8% limits (Figure 4). These estimates are adjusted only for the variables available in
the billing data.

## Change after 2020

The monthly series showed a level change in 2020 without a subsequent change in slope (Figure 3): OR 1.50 (95% CI
1.28–1.77) at March 2020 and 1.54 (1.33–1.79) at May 2020, the break point of minimum deviance (Table 3). Placebo
break points in 2014 and 2016 showed no change, but a smaller increase was already present in 2018 (OR 1.13,
1.02–1.25), indicating that the rise began before the pandemic. Comparing 2022–2025 with 2018–2019, the
adjusted OR was 1.45 (1.37–1.53), 1.64 (1.54–1.75) within hospitals, 1.51 (1.39–1.65) in hospitals with plausible
diagnostic coding, and 1.44–1.45 in all sensitivity analyses; it was present in rheumatic (1.35) and non-rheumatic
(1.56) disease. Annual biological implants in this age group fell by 12% and mechanical implants rose by 21%, with
total volume that fell in 2020–2021 and recovered thereafter (Table S6). The change occurred in both sexes and both positions; in women aged 15–44 years,
mechanical use rose from 40.2% to 56.7% (aortic) and from 40.3% to 52.9% (mitral) between 2018–2019 and 2022–2025
(Figure S2; Table S1). The change also occurred in hospitals that kept their main supplier of mechanical prostheses
(Table S5).

# DISCUSSION

In this 26-year national analysis of the Brazilian public health system, we found three main results. First,
unlike high-income countries, the SUS did not experience a marked decline in mechanical prostheses: the mechanical
share remained around one-third overall and close to one-half in patients younger than 60 years.
Second, prosthesis choice varied widely between hospitals and regions, more than most recorded patient
characteristics. Third, mechanical use in younger patients increased after 2020 and remained higher, reaching the
highest level of the series in the mitral position.

The contrast with the United States is striking. Between 2008 and 2017, mechanical prostheses fell to 17% of aortic
and 29% of mitral replacements in patients aged 50–70 years [11], and to about one-half and 40% in women aged
15–49 years [12]. Spain reported a similar decline [13]. In the SUS, the share in younger patients fell only
modestly during the 2010s and then rose. Current guidelines would support mechanical prostheses in most patients
younger than 60–65 years [1,2], and observational data from California, Sweden, New York and Korea suggest a
survival benefit of mechanical prostheses below these ages [4–8]; nationwide data from Uruguay
also showed higher mortality with biological aortic prostheses below 60 years [24,25]. Mechanical prosthesis use in patients younger than 60 years thus
remained closer to the age thresholds recommended by current guidelines than the rates observed in several
high-income countries. However, the same guidelines
recommend a biological prosthesis when adequate anticoagulation is unlikely [1,2], and access to INR monitoring
varies widely in Brazil [3,15]. Whether patients receiving mechanical prostheses in the SUS achieve adequate
anticoagulation cannot be assessed with these data.

The between-hospital variation (MOR 3.15) indicates that, for patients with the same recorded characteristics, the
odds of a mechanical prosthesis differed about three-fold between two randomly chosen hospitals; part of this may
reflect unrecorded clinical differences between hospitals. Regional variation in prosthesis choice was
described in the United States [11,26], but the magnitude here is large and persisted after adjustment for age,
aetiology and volume. The South, with the highest mechanical use, and the North and Northeast, with the lowest,
differ in rheumatic burden, socioeconomic profile and organization of cardiac surgery. A single-centre study in
Bahia attributed the preference for biological prostheses in young patients to socioeconomic barriers to
anticoagulation [18]; our regional pattern is consistent with that hypothesis, but unmeasured confounding remains likely and the
hypothesis requires direct study.

The increase after 2020 was abrupt, coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, persisted after 2022, occurred within
hospitals and was robust to all sensitivity analyses. It was not explained by the recorded case mix, coding or supplier, and a smaller increase was
already present in 2018. Absolute numbers of biological implants declined while mechanical implants increased;
whether this reflects a change in prosthesis preference, patient selection or hospital-level factors cannot be
determined from these data. Plausible explanations include changes in the supply and relative
reimbursement of prostheses, changes in surgical practice during the pandemic and a deliberate return to guideline
age thresholds. These data cannot distinguish them, and we report the change as an observation that requires
explanation.

The increase in mechanical prostheses in women aged 15–44 years deserves attention. Both guidelines favour a
biological prosthesis in women contemplating pregnancy [1,2], because pregnancy with a mechanical valve carries
substantial maternal and fetal risk [27]. In Sweden, one-half of young women with a biological aortic prosthesis
had at least one delivery, compared with 9.5% of those with a mechanical prosthesis [28]. In the SUS, more than
one-half of these women received a mechanical prosthesis in 2022–2025, while in the United States the proportion
declined [12]. Pregnancy intention, counselling and anticoagulation management are not recorded, and this finding
calls for further investigation of decision-making in this group.

## What these data cannot show

Several determinants of prosthesis choice that matter to surgeons are not recorded: the feasibility of mitral
repair, the Ross procedure as an alternative in young adults, anticipation of transcatheter valve-in-valve
procedures, the quality of INR control and patient preference. The data therefore describe what was implanted,
not why, and cannot judge whether individual choices were appropriate.

## Limitations

First, valve position and aetiology were derived from diagnostic codes. Some hospitals used a single default code,
and rheumatic disease was under-coded compared with a reference centre; results were unchanged after excluding these
hospitals or restricting to hospitals with plausible coding. Second, the data include only publicly funded
admissions; patients treated in the private sector, about one-quarter of the population, may differ. Third, clinical
variables (ventricular function, rhythm, comorbidities, patient preference, contraindication to anticoagulation)
are unavailable, so variation may partly reflect unmeasured case mix. Fourth, the public files lack a personal
identifier; we therefore did not analyse survival, reintervention or complications, and Table S7 is exploratory.
Fifth, data before 2008 come from an earlier coding system; continuity across 2007–2008 was confirmed, but position
is less complete in 2000–2001.

# CONCLUSION

Over 26 years, Brazil's public health system maintained substantial use of mechanical prostheses, with large
variation between hospitals and regions and an increase in patients younger than 60 years after 2020. Understanding the drivers of this pattern and the quality of anticoagulation in these patients is
a priority for valve care in middle-income countries.
